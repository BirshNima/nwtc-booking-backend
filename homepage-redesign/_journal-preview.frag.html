How early to book a SeaTac airport pickup, by time of day

The honest answer, from the people who dispatch these runs every morning: it depends on your departure time and where you're starting — but here's how to get it right.

Northwest Town Car Service · updated September 2026 · 6 min read

For a domestic flight out of Sea-Tac, plan to leave home about 2.5 hours before departure — roughly 90 minutes at the airport plus your drive. Add 30–45 minutes for an international flight, a holiday weekend, or a starting point more than 30 minutes from SEA. A good chauffeur builds that math into your pickup time so you don't have to.

The mistake people make is planning backward from "boarding" instead of "the moment security stops being predictable." SEA's TSA lines are usually fine — and occasionally 45 minutes. You want a buffer that absorbs the bad day without leaving you sitting at the gate for two hours on the good one. Here's how we set it.

The rule of thumb

Work back from your departure time in three pieces:

  • At the airport: 90 minutes for a domestic flight with a checked bag, 2 hours for international. If you're carry-on only and have TSA PreCheck or Clear, you can shave 20–30 minutes off the domestic number — but only if you're not also trying to grab breakfast.
  • The drive: your realistic door-to-curb time at that hour, not the Google estimate for an empty freeway. From most of the close-in cities that's 15–35 minutes off-peak and 30–55 in rush.
  • A cushion: 15 minutes. Traffic incidents, a slow elevator, a bag you forgot to zip.

Add those up and that's your pickup time. On our end, we also add a few minutes so the car is waiting when you walk out, not pulling up as you're locking the door.

By your departure time

Early morning (before 8 a.m.)

The freeways are clear and TSA opens light, so the drive is fast — but this is also when checkpoint staffing is thinnest and a single delayed line has nowhere to spill. For a 6 a.m. flight, we typically set pickup around 4:15–4:30 from the Eastside and the South Sound, a bit later from Kent and the close-in cities. Yes, it's early. It's also the flight people miss most.

Midday (10 a.m.–2 p.m.)

The sweet spot. Traffic is moderate, TSA is fully staffed, and the math is straightforward: departure minus 2.5 hours, minus your drive. A midday flight from Bellevue with a checked bag is usually a pickup about 3 hours ahead.

Afternoon & evening (3 p.m.–8 p.m.)

The drive is the wild card. Southbound I‑5 and I‑405 are unpredictable from about 3:30 to 6:30, and an evening event downtown or at Lumen Field can add 20 minutes to any route that touches the city. We pad the drive estimate hard for these and, when it's ugly, route around the freeway.

Red-eye & late night (after 9 p.m.)

Roads are empty and lines are short, so a red-eye needs the least lead time of any flight — often 90 minutes door to gate is plenty. The thing to confirm is that your airline's counter is still staffed if you have a bag to check; some close earlier than you'd expect.

International flights

Add a full hour over the domestic plan. International check-in and document verification take longer, the security and customs pre-clear process for some destinations is slower, and the international gates (the S concourse) are a train ride from the main terminal. For a 1 p.m. flight to Asia or Europe from the Eastside, a pickup around 9:30 a.m. is right.

The week around Thanksgiving, the days before Christmas, and spring-break Fridays turn SEA into a different airport — the garage fills, the curb is chaos, and security lines run 45–60 minutes. Add 45 minutes to your normal buffer, and consider a meet-and-greet so your driver handles the bags while you go straight in. The same goes for any day with a major event that snarls the freeways between you and the airport.

Arrivals are the easy direction. When we're picking you up from a flight, we track it and adjust — you don't set a time, you just tell us the flight number. Our airport rate includes 60 minutes of wait after landing, which covers a normal customs-and-bags experience with room to spare.

For departures, it doesn't help much — you still have to be there on time regardless of whether the plane is. For arrivals, it changes everything: your pickup time moves automatically with the flight, earlier or later, and there's no charge for the wait on a delayed arrival. It's the single best reason to book a car instead of standing at the rideshare pillar refreshing the app.

Bellevue to Sea-Tac: how long it really takes, by hour

Not the empty-freeway estimate — the times a dispatcher actually plans around, and why the answer changes by 25 minutes across the day.

Northwest Town Car Service · updated September 2026 · 5 min read

Bellevue to Sea-Tac is 25–35 minutes off-peak and 40–55 in weekday rush, and which of the two routes wins — I‑405 south to I‑5, or I‑90 west to I‑5 — depends entirely on the hour. For a morning flight, leaving Bellevue about 2.5 hours before departure is right most days; make it 3 for an early international flight or a Monday.

The two routes, and when each one wins

Almost every Bellevue airport trip uses one of two paths:

  • I‑405 south to I‑5 through Tukwila. The direct line. Its weak point is the Renton S‑curves, where 405 narrows and merges — a reliable slowdown from roughly 3:30 to 6:30 on weekdays, and unpredictable at other times.
  • I‑90 west to I‑5 south. A few miles longer but avoids the S‑curves. Its weak point is I‑90 westbound across Lake Washington, which backs up in the afternoon as the city empties out.

The practical rule: 405 in the morning, I‑90 in the late afternoon, and your driver checks both live before pickup regardless. From downtown Bellevue we get onto 405 via Bellevue Way and 112th rather than fighting the ramp at Main Street.

By time of day

Before 7 a.m. — 22–28 minutes, either route. This is the fast window. A 6 a.m. flight is a pickup around 3:30.

7–9 a.m. — 35–50 minutes. Southbound 405 and I‑5 both carry commute traffic. Leave with the full buffer.

9 a.m.–3 p.m. — 25–35 minutes. The reliable midday window; departure minus 2.5 hours minus the drive.

3:30–6:30 p.m. — 40–55 minutes, sometimes worse. The S‑curves and I‑90 westbound are both slow. An afternoon flight needs the same lead time as a morning one.

After 7 p.m. — back to 25–30 minutes.

What makes a normal day abnormal

Three things blow up the estimate: a collision anywhere on 405 or I‑5 between Bellevue and the airport (there's no good detour), an evening event at Lumen Field or Climate Pledge Arena that floods I‑5 and the SoDo surface streets, and the week around a major holiday. When any of those is in play we add 20–30 minutes and, if it's bad, route off the freeway entirely.

How we set your pickup

We work backward from your flight: 90 minutes at the airport for a domestic flight (2 hours international), plus the realistic drive for that hour, plus a 15‑minute cushion. Then we add a few minutes so the car is waiting when you walk out rather than pulling up as you lock the door. On a Bellevue airport account, the booker doesn't do this math — it's baked into the confirmed pickup time. The Bellevue service-area page has the flat rate ($89 sedan, $119 SUV) and the neighborhood pickup details.

SEA pickup: meet-and-greet, curbside, or the cell phone lot

Three ways to be picked up at Sea-Tac, what each one feels like on the ground, and when it's worth paying for the sign at baggage claim.

Northwest Town Car Service · updated September 2026 · 4 min read

For most trips, curbside on the arrivals drive is fastest — you clear the terminal, we're at the door. Meet-and-greet — a chauffeur waiting at baggage claim with your name on a sign — is worth it for international arrivals, first-time visitors, anyone travelling with a lot of luggage or kids, and any trip where a client is being received. The cell phone lot is a rideshare and private-car staging area, not really a car-service thing.

Curbside on the arrivals drive

How it works: when you're off the plane and near baggage claim, you text the driver (or we're already tracking the flight). You take the escalator or elevator down to the arrivals level — the lower roadway — and walk out to the third lane, the one for pickups. We pull up, load the bags, and go. From "I'm walking out" to "in the car" is usually 3–6 minutes.

Best for: domestic arrivals, carry-on or one checked bag, someone who's flown into SEA before. It's the default, and it's included in the airport rate.

Meet-and-greet at baggage claim

How it works: your chauffeur parks, walks into the terminal, and stands at your assigned baggage carousel holding a sign with your name (or your company's). They help with the bags and walk you to the car. You don't have to navigate anything or text anyone.

Best for: international arrivals (you're tired and the customs exit is disorienting), first-time visitors, anyone with kids or a wheelchair or a golf bag, and — importantly — any trip where you're receiving someone. A client walking out to a person holding their name is a different arrival than a client thumbing through an app at the rideshare pillar. On our airport service, meet-and-greet is included, not an upcharge.

The cell phone lot

SEA's cell phone lot is a free waiting area off Air Cargo Road where drivers stage until their passenger calls. It exists so people aren't circling the terminal. For a car service it's mostly irrelevant — your chauffeur is either already curbside on your text or inside for a meet-and-greet. If you're being picked up by a friend or family member, though, the cell phone lot is where they should wait rather than looping the drives.

How the choice affects timing

Curbside adds nothing — the driver times their approach to your text. Meet-and-greet means the driver needs to park and walk in before you reach baggage claim, so we watch the flight and head in as it lands. Either way, our airport rate includes 60 minutes of wait after landing, which covers a normal customs-and-bags experience with margin. If a bag is genuinely lost or customs is a mess, the driver waits and we sort the details later.

Snow, wind & SEA: how weather changes an airport pickup plan

The Puget Sound doesn't get much snow, but when it does, the region stops. Here's how a car service handles the days rideshare surges and disappears.

Northwest Town Car Service · updated September 2026 · 4 min read

On a bad-weather day, a car service does three things an app can't: it leaves earlier because the pickup is scheduled, it holds the price while rideshare surges 2× or more, and it stays committed to your trip instead of the driver cancelling for a better fare. For a flight you can't miss, the storm day is exactly when the reservation earns its keep.

Snow and ice: the region's weak spot

Seattle gets snow a few times a winter, and because it's rare, the hills and the untreated side streets become the problem — not the freeways, which get plowed. A chauffeur who knows the area routes around the steep neighborhood streets, allows double or triple the normal drive time, and on a genuine ice event will suggest an earlier pickup or, for a non-urgent trip, a different day. We'd rather move your 6 a.m. pickup to 3:30 than have you sitting on I‑5 behind a spun-out car.

Wind: the flight problem, not the drive problem

Windstorms rarely slow the drive to SEA much, but they delay and cancel flights, and they knock out power — which can mean traffic signals down on the arterials between you and the airport. For arrivals, flight tracking handles the delay automatically. For departures, we watch your flight status and keep in touch, because a two-hour delay changes when you actually want to leave.

Fog: mostly an arrivals story

Fog at SEA causes ground stops and diversions — occasionally a flight lands in Portland or Spokane instead. If that happens on an arrival we're handling, we track it and either wait, adjust to the new landing time, or arrange the onward leg. You're not stranded figuring it out alone.

Why the flat rate matters most on these days

The 5 a.m. of the season's one snow day is precisely when rideshare is 2–3× and cars are scarce. A flat car-service rate is the same $89 from Bellevue it always is, and the driver is committed to your trip. The value of a fixed price isn't the average day — it's the day everything else breaks.

Corporate car account vs. rideshare for business travel

The honest comparison — where an account genuinely wins, and where a rideshare app is still the right call.

Northwest Town Car Service · updated September 2026 · 5 min read

For a company doing more than a handful of car trips a month — airport runs, client pickups, roadshows — a corporate account with a car service usually costs about the same per trip as rideshare once you account for surge, and it removes the two things that actually cost you: no-shows at the worst possible moment, and a month of receipts to reconcile.

Rideshare is excellent for what it's for: a spontaneous ride, one person, low stakes. Business travel is often none of those things. When a partner has a 6 a.m. flight, when a client is landing and expects to be met, when the CFO is doing four cities in three days — the failure modes of an app (no cars, surge, a driver who cancels, a car that's "3 minutes away" for fifteen minutes) go from annoying to expensive. Here's the real breakdown.

Cost, honestly

On a calm Tuesday afternoon, a rideshare to Sea-Tac from downtown Seattle might run $35–45 and a black-car flat rate is $89. That gap is real, and for a single low-stakes trip, the app wins.

But business travel doesn't happen only on calm Tuesdays. It clusters around early mornings, Friday afternoons, weather events, and conference weeks — exactly when rideshare surges 1.5–2.5×. That same downtown-to-SEA trip is $70–110 on a surging morning, with no cap and no warning until you're standing at the curb. A flat corporate rate is $89 at 4 a.m. on the Friday before Thanksgiving, same as always. Over a month of business travel, the flat rate and the blended rideshare cost land close — and the flat rate is predictable, which finance departments value more than a few dollars saved.

Reliability and the no-show problem

The scenario that justifies an account on its own: a driver accepts your executive's 5:15 a.m. pickup, then cancels at 5:10 because a better fare appeared, and now there are no cars in the neighborhood. Your partner misses the flight. That trip didn't cost $40 — it cost a rebooked itinerary and a missed meeting.

A car service account is a scheduled commitment. The pickup is assigned to a specific driver the day before, the driver confirms, and dispatch has a backup if something goes wrong. For a ride where being on time is the entire point, that structure is the product.

The arrivals case is even clearer. When a client lands at SEA expecting a car, "your driver will be 8 minutes" is not an acceptable answer. A car service tracks the flight, has a driver staged, and — with meet-and-greet — has someone at baggage claim with the client's name on a sign. That's a relationship investment an app can't make.

Expense reporting

Every rideshare trip is a separate transaction, a separate receipt, a separate line for someone to categorize and for someone else to approve. Multiply by a travelling team and it's real administrative time each month, plus the inevitable missing receipts and the "was this ride personal?" questions.

A corporate account is one itemized invoice a month, on net terms, with every trip tied to a booker and — if you use them — a cost code or department. Finance gets a single document to review and pay. For companies with expense-policy scrutiny, that alone can justify the switch.

Duty of care

If your company sends employees on the road, you carry some responsibility for how they get around. A licensed carrier — commercial insurance, permitted and vetted drivers, a real business you can call — is a defensible answer to that. A gig driver in a personal car with rideshare's insurance is a grayer one. For international visitors, junior employees travelling alone, or late-night trips, many companies decide the professional service is the responsible default.

When rideshare is still the right call

Be honest about this: for a single employee grabbing a quick, off-peak, low-stakes ride across town, the app is faster to summon and cheaper. A good corporate travel policy uses both — the account for airport transfers, client-facing trips, executive travel, roadshows, and anything early-morning or weather-sensitive; the app for the incidental daytime hop. The account doesn't have to replace rideshare to pay for itself; it just has to cover the trips where failure is expensive.

Booking transportation for a Seattle conference or roadshow

Multi-day, multi-vehicle, multiple stakeholders — the kind of ground transportation that goes wrong in small ways unless one person owns it.

Northwest Town Car Service · updated September 2026 · 5 min read

For a Seattle conference, investor roadshow, or corporate event, book through a single account with one coordinator rather than assembling rides trip by trip. You give the schedule; they build the vehicle plan, assign consistent drivers, and send one itemized invoice. Start the conversation three to four weeks out for anything with more than a couple of vehicles.

Roadshows: the fast, tight version

An investor roadshow is a sequence of meetings, often four to six a day, with a principal or two and a banker, moving between offices in downtown Seattle and Bellevue on a schedule that can't slip. What it needs: one executive sedan or SUV held for the full day (or two days), the same chauffeur throughout so they learn the rhythm, and a driver who knows which garage to use at each building and how long the I‑90 or 520 crossing actually takes. Book it as an hourly / as-directed engagement so the car stays with the team between stops.

Conferences: the volume version

A conference brings speakers, VIPs, and staff in and out of SEA over two or three days, plus airport-to-hotel and hotel-to-venue movement. What it needs: a schedule of arrivals and departures matched to flight numbers, a mix of sedans for individuals and Sprinter vans for groups, and a point of contact who can absorb the inevitable changes — a flight that shifts, a speaker who adds a dinner. This is an account, not a series of one-off bookings.

One point of contact

The thing that makes event transportation work is that your team calls or texts one number — the coordinator who has the whole schedule — rather than a general dispatch line. Changes go to that person, they cascade the adjustments, and nobody on your side is chasing individual drivers.

Billing

Everything runs on the account and lands as a single itemized invoice — by trip, by passenger, and by cost code if you use them — on net terms. No expense reports from a dozen rideshare receipts, no reconciling whose ride was whose. For a recurring event or a company that runs roadshows regularly, a standing corporate account with a negotiated rate makes it repeatable.

  • 3–4 weeks out: share the draft schedule and headcounts; get a vehicle plan and quote; hold the vehicles.
  • 1 week out: confirm the final schedule, flight numbers, and the on-site contact.
  • Day of: the coordinator runs it; your team texts one number for changes.
  • After: one invoice.

What's in the car

A clean, well-kept vehicle — inspected and cleaned between trips — with bottled water and Wi-Fi. It's quiet enough to take a call or work. You choose how much conversation you want; a good chauffeur reads that and follows your lead.

Seattle wedding transportation timeline: a planner's checklist

What to lock in and when — from the first vehicle count to the send-off — so the transportation is the one vendor nobody worries about on the day.

Northwest Town Car Service · updated September 2026 · 6 min read

Book your full wedding transportation two to three months out for a peak-season Saturday — earlier for summer dates, which fill first. A single couple's car can often be arranged with a few weeks' notice, but shuttles and multi-vehicle plans need lead time. Everything below is the order a coordinator works in.

3–4 months out: the rough plan

You don't need every detail yet — you need the shape. Sketch the day: where the couple gets ready, the ceremony site, the reception venue, and where out-of-town guests are staying. Count heads for anything that needs a shuttle. Get a preliminary quote based on that shape so you know the budget, and put a deposit down to hold vehicles for a summer Saturday.

6–8 weeks out: the run-of-show

Now the times firm up. Work backward from the ceremony start:

  • Couple & wedding party — pickup with enough margin for photos and the inevitable delay. A first look changes this.
  • Family & VIPs — parents and grandparents, often a separate car so they're not on the party's schedule.
  • Guest shuttles — hotel-to-venue loops timed to have everyone seated 15 minutes before the ceremony, with a return loop or two after the reception ends.
  • The couple's exit — the getaway car, and where it stages so the send-off works.

Hand this to your transportation coordinator and they'll turn it into a written timeline with every pickup, staging point, and buffer.

2–3 weeks out: confirm and adjust

Final guest count, final vehicle mix, final timeline. This is when you catch the things that shift a plan: a venue that only allows drop-offs at a specific gate, a photo location added between the ceremony and reception, a rehearsal-dinner car for the night before. Confirm the balance payment and any decoration (magnetic signs and ribbon are fine; agree anything adhesive now).

The week of: the details that matter day-of

Your coordinator confirms every leg and gives you and your planner a single point of contact who's reachable on the day. Share the venue's loading and parking rules, any gate codes, and the name of the person the drivers should check in with. Decide who's responsible for telling the shuttle when the reception is actually wrapping — it's never exactly the printed end time.

On the day

If the plan is built right, this section is short. Cars arrive early. The couple's driver knows the exit. Shuttles run their loops. Overtime, if the reception genuinely runs long, is added at the vehicle's hourly rate and your coordinator confirms it with you rather than surprising you on the invoice. You dance; we drive.

Getting from SEA to the Seattle cruise terminals

Pier 66 or Pier 91, how long the drive really is, when to actually show up, and how the return morning works.

Northwest Town Car Service · updated September 2026 · 5 min read

Seattle has two cruise terminals — Pier 66 (Bell Street, downtown) and Pier 91 (Smith Cove, in Interbay). Your cruise line assigns which one, and it's printed on your boarding documents. From Sea-Tac, both are a 20–40 minute drive depending on traffic, and the single most important thing is to arrive within your assigned boarding window — not before it.

Which pier is yours?

Don't guess — check your cruise documents. Pier assignments shift from year to year and even between sailings. As a rough guide for recent Alaska seasons:

  • Pier 91 (Smith Cove Cruise Terminal, ~2001 W Garfield St) — the larger terminal, generally used by the bigger lines and most of the Alaska fleet.
  • Pier 66 (Bell Street Cruise Terminal, ~2225 Alaskan Way) — downtown at the foot of Bell Street, generally used by Norwegian and Oceania sailings.

The two piers are about 10 minutes apart, so a mix-up isn't a disaster — but it is 10 minutes you didn't budget on a tight morning.

Drive time from SEA

ToDistanceTypical drive
Pier 66 (downtown)~14 mi20–35 min
Pier 91 (Interbay)~16 mi25–40 min

Both routes run up SR‑99 or I‑5 into downtown; Pier 91 continues north on Elliott and 15th Ave W. The wild card is turnaround Saturday in Alaska season (roughly May through late September), when several ships sail the same day and the streets around both piers fill with taxis, buses, and drop-offs from late morning through mid-afternoon. Your driver knows the staging patterns and the least-bad approach, but on a peak Saturday, add 15 minutes to the numbers above.

When to arrive

Cruise lines assign staggered boarding times — you'll have a window like "12:30–1:30 p.m." on your documents. This exists to prevent everyone showing up at once. If you arrive before your window, you'll wait outside the terminal, often standing, sometimes in the rain. If you arrive after it, you're fine as long as it's before the "all aboard" time, usually 3:00–3:30 p.m. for a 4:00 departure.

The move: aim for the start of your window, plus a small buffer. If your window opens at 12:30 and you're coming from SEA, a pickup around 11:30 lands you there right as it opens, bags dropped and through security before the crowd. We time your pickup to your window, not to the ship's departure.

Flying in the same day? It's doable but tight — aim to land by 10 a.m. for an afternoon sailing, and build in margin for a delayed flight and baggage. Many cruisers fly in the night before and stay at a downtown hotel; if that's you, we'll do the hotel-to-pier leg in the morning, which is a 5–15 minute trip.

Luggage at the pier

At both terminals, porters take your checked bags at the curb — you hand them over, they go to your stateroom, and you carry on only what you need for the afternoon (documents, medications, a change of clothes in case bags are slow). A tip of a few dollars per bag is customary. Your chauffeur helps move everything from the trunk to the porter, so you're not wrestling suitcases across a busy drop-off lane.

The return morning

Disembarkation is the mirror image and, honestly, slower. The ship docks around 6:30–7:00 a.m., and you leave in groups by luggage tag color, typically between 7:00 and 9:30, then clear customs. You won't know your exact off-time until the last night aboard, when the crew posts the schedule.

Two ways to handle the pickup:

  • Book a set time based on your assigned disembarkation group — usually 30–45 minutes after your group is called, to allow for customs.
  • Text us when you're off the ship and through customs, and we send the car. On a cruise-terminal return we track the ship's actual arrival and stage a driver nearby, so the wait is short either way — and there's no charge for our wait.

From the pier, it's the same 20–40 minutes back to SEA. If your flight home is before noon, give yourself a comfortable margin — a customs delay plus the drive plus check-in adds up fast.

A Woodinville wine tour itinerary for a relaxed half day

Four districts, forty-plus tasting rooms, and a strong temptation to over-schedule. Here's a sensible route through three or four rooms with lunch — and the practical details.

Northwest Town Car Service · updated September 2026 · 6 min read

Woodinville is 30–40 minutes from Seattle, 15–20 from the Eastside, and home to more than 40 tasting rooms grouped into four districts. For a good half day, plan on three to four rooms over four to five hours, including lunch — not six rooms in a march. The rooms are close together, so the driving is short; the pacing is what makes or breaks it.

The four districts

  • Hollywood District — near the Sammamish River, anchored by Chateau Ste. Michelle's château and grounds, Columbia Winery across the street, and DeLille Cellars' winery and restaurant. This is the "estate" end — bigger properties, gardens, more of a destination feel.
  • Warehouse District — the densest concentration, in an industrial park off Woodinville‑Redmond Road. Dozens of small producers in clustered suites you can walk between. Less polish, more discovery, and the best odds for a walk-in.
  • West Valley — along the Woodinville‑Redmond Road corridor, a spread of mid-sized rooms and the Hollywood Schoolhouse events venue.
  • Downtown Woodinville (the Schoolhouse District) — the newer, walkable development around the old schoolhouse, with tasting rooms, restaurants, and a distillery or two mixed in.

You don't need to hit all four. A relaxed day picks one anchor and one cluster — say, Chateau Ste. Michelle in Hollywood plus a few rooms in the Warehouse District — with a real lunch in between.

A half-day route (roughly noon to 5)

The relaxed version

11:45Pickup. It's a short drive, so a late-morning start still gives you a full afternoon.
12:15Hollywood District anchor — the château grounds and a tasting at Chateau Ste. Michelle or DeLille. Big, easy first stop; walk the gardens.
1:30Lunch. Sit-down at Barking Frog or the Willows Lodge dining room, or something quicker in downtown Woodinville. Eating properly at the midpoint is the whole trick.
2:45Warehouse District, room one — a small producer you've been curious about. Book this one ahead.
3:45Warehouse District, room two — walk next door; this is where a walk-in usually works.
4:45Optional third room, or call it and head home while everyone's still having a good time.
5:15Departure. Home by 6, which is a much better outcome than closing the place down.

If you'd rather do the estate end entirely — Chateau Ste. Michelle, Columbia, JM Cellars, DeLille — that's a fine four-stop day too, with less walking and more grounds.

Reservations or walk-ins?

The rules have tightened. Reserve ahead for weekends, for anything in the Hollywood or Downtown districts, and for the well-known names — many now require it and cap group sizes. The Warehouse District is more forgiving: smaller rooms, more walk-in flexibility, especially on a weekday afternoon. If your group is more than four people, call ahead everywhere. Most rooms are open Thursday through Sunday; weekday hours are thinner, so check before you build a Tuesday around it.

Tasting fees run roughly $15–35 a person per room, often waived or credited with a bottle purchase. Budget for it, and budget a little for the bottles you'll want to take home — there's room in the trunk, and your driver will help load.

Getting there, and why a driver

From Seattle it's I‑5 or SR‑99 to SR‑520 to the Woodinville exits, about 30–40 minutes. From Bellevue and Redmond it's 15–20 up the Sammamish River valley. From Kent and the South Sound, closer to 45–55.

The case for hiring a car is simple: a wine tour has no designated driver by design. Everyone tastes, nobody watches the clock or the road, and the day ends when you want it to rather than when someone has to switch to water. We book these by the hour — the sedan is $95/hr, the SUV $125, the Sprinter van $225 for a bigger group — with a three-hour minimum that a Woodinville half day clears easily. The driver parks, waits, and carries the cases.
