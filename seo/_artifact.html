01 Strategy

Three commitments shape everything below.

Commercial intent over traffic

A first page for "how to tip a chauffeur" is worthless; page three for "Bellevue airport car service" prints money. Every target term is one a person searches when they're close to booking a ride. Informational content exists only to support those commercial pages and to build topical authority — never as a traffic goal in itself.

Local before national

"Seattle limo service" is contested by national aggregators with huge budgets. "Redmond car service", "SEA to Woodinville wine tour", and the map pack for "airport car service near me" are winnable with a real local operator, a complete Google Business Profile, and genuinely useful city pages. Win those first; the broad head terms follow from the authority they build.

Measured to revenue

Rankings are a leading indicator, not the goal. The scorecard in §11 tracks the full chain: impressions → clicks → leads → quotes → bookings → revenue, by landing page and by query. A page that ranks but doesn't produce leads gets fixed or cut.

What we will not do

No doorway pages (near-identical city pages with the name swapped), no keyword stuffing, no fabricated location signals (addresses or "offices" that don't exist), no purchased links, no fake reviews. Google's local algorithm punishes all of these and they're reputational risks for a licensed carrier.

05 Location pages — the discipline

Location pages are the highest-ROI build and the easiest to get wrong. The line between a ranking asset and a Google penalty is whether the page says something true and specific that only applies to that city.

Build in this order

  1. Kent — home base. The strongest page: this is where the business is, real local knowledge, the $79 rate.
  2. Bellevue, Redmond, Kirkland — Eastside, highest corporate + airport value, real drive-time and route detail to write.
  3. Tacoma, Renton, Everett — distinct South/North Sound markets with their own airport rate and route story.
  4. The rest (Auburn, Federal Way, Puyallup, Lynnwood, Edmonds, Gig Harbor) — only when someone can write 300+ words of real local content. Until then they live as rows in the homepage coverage grid, which is enough to rank for the long tail without a thin page.
The find-and-replace test

Copy the draft, replace every instance of the city name with a different city, and read it again. If it still makes sense, it's a doorway page — rewrite it with real specifics (routes, neighborhoods, landmarks, drive times, local demand) or don't ship it. Ten strong city pages beat forty weak ones.

Every city page must have

  • The city's actual SEA flat rate (sedan + SUV), pulled from the pricing table
  • Real drive time to SEA — typical and rush-hour — and the route(s)
  • Named pickup areas / neighborhoods and any local quirk (one-way streets, hotel loading zones, HOA gates)
  • One line on what that city's demand skews toward (corporate, airport, events)
  • Service schema with areaServed = that city
  • Links to the 3 main service pages and 1–2 neighboring city pages

06 Internal-link model

Hub and spoke. Link equity flows from the homepage to the service pages (the hubs), and between service pages, city pages, and the blog posts that support them.

Homepage → all 6 service pages + 4 priority city pages
Service page → 2–3 sibling service pages · its supporting blog posts · 2–4 relevant city pages
City page → the 3 core service pages · 1–2 neighboring city pages · homepage
Blog post → its anchor service page (twice: intro + conclusion) · 1 related post · homepage

Rules

  • Descriptive anchor text, never "click here" — "flat SEA airport rates", "set up a corporate account", "Bellevue car service".
  • Every new page gets at least one link from an existing indexed page within a week of publishing, or it won't get crawled promptly.
  • The footer links every service page and the priority city pages site-wide — that's the safety net.
  • No page more than 3 clicks from the homepage.

08 Local SEO & Google Business Profile

For a car service, the GBP is the single highest-leverage asset — it owns the map pack and every "near me" search. Treat it like a second homepage.

Profile setup

  • Primary category: "Limousine service" or "Car service" — test which the map pack favors for your target terms. Secondary: Airport shuttle service, Chauffeur service, Transportation service.
  • Service area business (no storefront) — set the service area to the cities in §3, not a radius. Hide the street address if it's residential.
  • Services — add each of the 6 with a 1–2 sentence description echoing the page (airport transfers, corporate accounts, weddings, hourly, point-to-point, cruise & wine tours).
  • Attributes — 24/7, wheelchair-accessible if true, LGBTQ-friendly, veteran/family-owned if applicable.
  • Photos — real vehicles (exterior + interior), a chauffeur, the SEA arrivals area. Add a few every month; freshness is a ranking factor.
  • Q&A — seed 6–8 of your own questions and answers (rates, wait time, flight tracking, service area), then monitor for new ones.

Reviews — the compounding asset

  • Target: 2–4 new reviews a week. The trip-completion automation (CRM §5) sends the request; 4–5★ riders get a direct Google link.
  • Respond to every review within 48 hours — thank the positives by name and trip type ("glad the 5 a.m. SEA run went smoothly"), address the negatives calmly and offline.
  • Reviews that mention a city or service ("great Bellevue airport pickup") reinforce that keyword — the follow-up message can gently prompt for specifics.

NAP consistency

One exact name, phone, and city string everywhere: Northwest Town Car Service · (206) 596-5504 · Kent, WA. Audit and fix every existing listing before building new ones — inconsistency is the most common local-ranking drag.

Weekly GBP posts

One post a week — a service highlight, a seasonal note ("holiday travel books up early"), a new blog post, a review screenshot. Keeps the profile active and gives the map listing more surface area.

09 Citations, links & digital PR

For a local service business, a handful of relevant, real links beats a large volume of generic ones. Everything here is earned or genuinely reciprocal.

Core citations (do first)

Consistent NAP on: Google Business Profile, Bing Places, Apple Business Connect, Yelp, Facebook, Nextdoor, the Better Business Bureau, and the WA UTC carrier directory. Then the transportation-relevant ones: Groundlink / affiliate networks, GroundWidgets, limo directories that vet operators.

Local partnerships (the real link source)

  • Hotels & venues — Seattle and Bellevue hotels, Woodinville wineries, wedding venues, event spaces. Offer to be their recommended transportation; ask for a link on their "getting here" or "preferred vendors" page. The wedding and city pages naming venues make this an easy ask.
  • Corporate travel & EA networks — local executive-assistant associations, coworking spaces, the chambers of commerce (Seattle, Bellevue, Kent, Tacoma). Sponsor or present; get the member-directory link.
  • Wedding vendors — planners, photographers, florists. Vendor cross-referral pages are common and links flow both ways.
  • Cruise & travel — travel agencies that book Alaska cruises out of Seattle.

Digital PR (opportunistic, not a program)

  • Respond to journalist requests (Connectively / Qwoted / HARO) on Seattle travel, SEA airport, holiday travel, and small-business topics — you're a quotable local operator.
  • Seasonal local-news angles: SEA holiday-travel crush, cruise-season logistics, prom safety. Pitch the local outlets and neighborhood blogs.
  • A genuinely useful free resource — an "SEA pickup timing" guide or a printable cruise-terminal cheat sheet — earns links on its own.
Avoid

Paid link packages, "guest post" farms, directory blasts, link exchanges with unrelated sites, and anything offering "100 backlinks". These carry real penalty risk and waste budget.

10 Technical checklist

  1. Google Search Console — verify the domain property, submit the sitemap, and set up email alerts. This is the source of truth for the scorecard.
  2. XML sitemap — auto-generated (Yoast / Rank Math on WordPress), all live pages, no noindex URLs, resubmitted when pages are added.
  3. robots.txt — allow crawling, disallow /book thank-you/confirmation URLs and any admin paths, link the sitemap.
  4. Canonical tags — self-referencing on every page; make sure WordPress isn't generating duplicate URLs (with/without trailing slash, ?replytocom, tag/date archives).
  5. Titles & meta descriptions — unique per page, from the briefs in §4. No page left on the WordPress default.
  6. Schema — LimousineService on the homepage, Service (+ FAQPage where there's an on-page FAQ) on every service and city page. Validate each with the Rich Results Test.
  7. Core Web Vitals — the new pages are near-static and should pass; check the live WordPress build for render-blocking scripts, unoptimized hero images, and layout shift. Serve images as WebP, lazy-load below the fold.
  8. Mobile — the pages are mobile-first; confirm tap targets, the sticky call button, and that the rate tables scroll rather than overflow.
  9. Redirects — 301 every old URL (the current homepage, any legacy service or contact pages) to its new equivalent. No chains, no 404s from external links.
  10. Internal search & thin pages — noindex WordPress tag pages, author archives, and the internal search results; they dilute crawl budget and can look like doorway pages.
  11. HTTPS + HSTS, one canonical host (https://northwesttowncarservice.com, www redirecting), and a clean 404 page that links back to the services.

11 Measurement

The plan is judged on leads and fares, so the tracking has to connect a search to a booking.

impression → click → lead → quote → booking → completed fare

Instrumentation

  • GA4 events (via GTM): phone_click, quote_start, quote_complete, booking_reserved, quote_request — the funnel already emits the data; wire these as the conversions.
  • Search Console — impressions, clicks, average position, and CTR by query and by page, exported monthly.
  • Call tracking (CallRail or similar) — a tracking number on the site so phone leads carry a source; SEO calls attributed to organic.
  • CRM — intake.js already stamps source, medium, and landing page on every lead. Filter the CRM by source = organic to see SEO leads, their status, and their value.
